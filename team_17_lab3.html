Part I: Parameter estimation¶

Aim: In Problems 1 and 2 you have to verify that the interval estimates produced by the known rules really contain the parameter with probability equal to the confidence level.

Problem 1¶

The expected value of the exponential distribution $\mathscr{E}(\lambda)$ is $1/\lambda$, so the sample mean $\overline{\mathbf{X}}$ is a good point estimate of the parameter $\theta := 1/\lambda$. A confidence interval for $\theta$ can be formed in several different ways:

  1. Using the exact distribution of the statistic $2\lambda n\overline{\mathbf{X}}$ (show that it is $\chi^2_{2n}$, then use the quantiles of the latter to get the interval endpoints).

  2. Using the normal approximation $\mathscr{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ for $\overline{\mathbf{X}}$, with parameters $\mu = \theta$ and $\sigma^2 = s^2/n$, where $s^2 = \theta^2$ is the population variance (the variance of the original distribution $\mathscr{E}(\lambda)$). In other words, form the $Z$-statistic $Z := \sqrt{n}\,(\overline{\mathbf{X}} - \theta)/\theta$ and use the fact that it is approximately standard normal $\mathscr{N}(0, 1)$:

    $$\mathsf{P}\big(|\theta - \overline{\mathbf{X}}| \le z_\beta\, \theta/\sqrt{n}\big) = \mathsf{P}(|Z| \le z_\beta) = 2\beta - 1,$$

    so $\theta$ lies within $\overline{\mathbf{X}} \pm z_\beta\,\theta/\sqrt{n}$ with probability $2\beta - 1$. (Here $z_\beta$ is the $\beta$-quantile of $\mathscr{N}(0, 1)$).

  3. The confidence interval constructed above uses the unknown variance $s^2 = \theta^2$, so it is of little use in practice. Instead, solve the double inequality

    $$|\theta - \overline{\mathbf{X}}| \le z_\beta\, \theta/\sqrt{n}$$

    for $\theta$. This gives another confidence interval that does not depend on the unknown parameter.

  4. Another, more universal way to remove the dependence on $\theta$ in (2) is to estimate $s$ by the sample standard deviation and approximate $\overline{\mathbf{X}}$ with the Student $t$-distribution (see Ross's textbook on statistics or the lecture notes for details).

Task (a): Derivation of Confidence Intervals¶

Let $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n \sim \mathscr{E}(\lambda)$ be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables.
The expectation is $\mathsf{E}[X_i] = \theta = \frac{1}{\lambda}$, and the population variance is $\mathrm{Var}(X_i) = \theta^2 = \frac{1}{\lambda^2}$.
The sample mean is defined as $\overline{\mathbf{X}} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n X_i$.

We construct a confidence interval for $\theta$ with confidence level $1 - \alpha$. Let $z_{1-\alpha/2}$ denote the $(1-\alpha/2)$-quantile of the standard normal distribution $\mathscr{N}(0, 1)$.

1. Exact Distribution using $\chi^2_{2n}$¶

Proof that $2\lambda n \overline{\mathbf{X}} \sim \chi^2_{2n}$:

The probability density function (PDF) of $X_i \sim \mathscr{E}(\lambda)$ is: $$ f_{X_i}(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}, \quad x \ge 0 $$

The Moment Generating Function (MGF) of $X_i$ is: $$ M_{X_i}(t) = \mathsf{E}[e^{t X_i}] = \int_0^\infty e^{tx} \lambda e^{-\lambda x} \, dx = \frac{\lambda}{\lambda - t} = \left( 1 - \frac{t}{\lambda} \right)^{-1}, \quad \text{for } t < \lambda $$

Let $S_n = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i = n\overline{\mathbf{X}}$. Since $X_i$ are independent, the MGF of $S_n$ is the product of individual MGFs: $$ M_{S_n}(t) = \prod_{i=1}^n M_{X_i}(t) = \left( 1 - \frac{t}{\lambda} \right)^{-n} $$

Now define the random variable $Q = 2\lambda n \overline{\mathbf{X}} = 2\lambda S_n$. The MGF of $Q$ is: $$ M_Q(t) = \mathsf{E}[e^{t Q}] = \mathsf{E}[e^{(2\lambda t) S_n}] = M_{S_n}(2\lambda t) = \left( 1 - \frac{2\lambda t}{\lambda} \right)^{-n} = (1 - 2t)^{-n} = (1 - 2t)^{-(2n)/2} $$

Recall that a Chi-square distribution with $k$ degrees of freedom, $\chi^2_k$, has the MGF $M_{\chi^2_k}(t) = (1 - 2t)^{-k/2}$.
Comparing the MGF of $Q$ with $M_{\chi^2_k}(t)$, we see that $Q$ has a Chi-square distribution with $k = 2n$ degrees of freedom: $$ Q = 2\lambda n \overline{\mathbf{X}} = \frac{2n\overline{\mathbf{X}}}{\theta} \sim \chi^2_{2n} $$

Derivation of the Interval:

Let $\chi^2_{2n, \alpha/2}$ and $\chi^2_{2n, 1-\alpha/2}$ be the $(\alpha/2)$-quantile and $(1-\alpha/2)$-quantile of $\chi^2_{2n}$, respectively.
The probability statement is: $$ \mathsf{P}\left( \chi^2_{2n, \alpha/2} \le \frac{2n\overline{\mathbf{X}}}{\theta} \le \chi^2_{2n, 1-\alpha/2} \right) = 1 - \alpha $$

Inverting the double inequality for $\theta$: $$ \chi^2_{2n, \alpha/2} \le \frac{2n\overline{\mathbf{X}}}{\theta} \implies \theta \le \frac{2n\overline{\mathbf{X}}}{\chi^2_{2n, \alpha/2}} $$ $$ \frac{2n\overline{\mathbf{X}}}{\theta} \le \chi^2_{2n, 1-\alpha/2} \implies \theta \ge \frac{2n\overline{\mathbf{X}}}{\chi^2_{2n, 1-\alpha/2}} $$

Thus, the exact $(1-\alpha)$ confidence interval for $\theta$ is: $$ \mathbf{CI}_1 = \left[ \frac{2n\overline{\mathbf{X}}}{\chi^2_{2n, 1-\alpha/2}}, \, \frac{2n\overline{\mathbf{X}}}{\chi^2_{2n, \alpha/2}} \right] $$

2. Normal Approximation with Population Variance $\sigma^2 = \theta^2$¶

By the Central Limit Theorem (CLT), for large $n$: $$ \overline{\mathbf{X}} \approx \mathscr{N}\left( \theta, \, \frac{\theta^2}{n} \right) $$

Forming the $Z$-statistic: $$ Z = \frac{\overline{\mathbf{X}} - \theta}{\theta / \sqrt{n}} \approx \mathscr{N}(0, 1) $$

With probability $1 - \alpha$: $$ \mathsf{P}\left( -z_{1-\alpha/2} \le \frac{\overline{\mathbf{X}} - \theta}{\theta / \sqrt{n}} \le z_{1-\alpha/2} \right) = 1 - \alpha $$

Isolating $\theta$ in the numerator: $$ -z_{1-\alpha/2} \frac{\theta}{\sqrt{n}} \le \overline{\mathbf{X}} - \theta \le z_{1-\alpha/2} \frac{\theta}{\sqrt{n}} $$ $$ \overline{\mathbf{X}} - z_{1-\alpha/2} \frac{\theta}{\sqrt{n}} \le \theta \le \overline{\mathbf{X}} + z_{1-\alpha/2} \frac{\theta}{\sqrt{n}} $$

This yields the confidence interval: $$ \mathbf{CI}_2 = \left[ \overline{\mathbf{X}} - z_{1-\alpha/2} \frac{\theta}{\sqrt{n}}, \, \overline{\mathbf{X}} + z_{1-\alpha/2} \frac{\theta}{\sqrt{n}} \right] $$

Note: The endpoints of $\mathbf{CI}_2$ depend on the unknown parameter $\theta$, which renders it a theoretical baseline rather than a practical interval.

3. Solving the Double Inequality for $\theta$¶

To remove the dependence on $\theta$ in Method 2, we solve the inequality for $\theta$: $$ |\theta - \overline{\mathbf{X}}| \le z_{1-\alpha/2} \frac{\theta}{\sqrt{n}} $$

This is equivalent to: $$ -z_{1-\alpha/2} \frac{\theta}{\sqrt{n}} \le \theta - \overline{\mathbf{X}} \le z_{1-\alpha/2} \frac{\theta}{\sqrt{n}} $$

We break this into two separate linear inequalities:

  1. Upper Bound: $$ \theta - \overline{\mathbf{X}} \le z_{1-\alpha/2} \frac{\theta}{\sqrt{n}} \implies \theta \left( 1 - \frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}}{\sqrt{n}} \right) \le \overline{\mathbf{X}} $$ For $n > z_{1-\alpha/2}^2$, the factor $\left( 1 - \frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}}{\sqrt{n}} \right) > 0$, so dividing gives: $$ \theta \le \frac{\overline{\mathbf{X}}}{1 - \frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}}{\sqrt{n}}} $$

  2. Lower Bound: $$ -z_{1-\alpha/2} \frac{\theta}{\sqrt{n}} \le \theta - \overline{\mathbf{X}} \implies \overline{\mathbf{X}} \le \theta \left( 1 + \frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}}{\sqrt{n}} \right) $$ Dividing by $\left( 1 + \frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}}{\sqrt{n}} \right) > 0$ yields: $$ \theta \ge \frac{\overline{\mathbf{X}}}{1 + \frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}}{\sqrt{n}}} $$

Combining both bounds gives the parameter-free confidence interval: $$ \mathbf{CI}_3 = \left[ \frac{\overline{\mathbf{X}}}{1 + \frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}}{\sqrt{n}}}, \, \frac{\overline{\mathbf{X}}}{1 - \frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}}{\sqrt{n}}} \right] $$

4. Student $t$-Distribution with Sample Standard Deviation¶

Replacing the population standard deviation $\theta$ with the sample standard deviation $s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \overline{\mathbf{X}})^2}$, we define the $T$-statistic: $$ T = \frac{\overline{\mathbf{X}} - \theta}{s / \sqrt{n}} $$

Approximating $T$ using Student's $t$-distribution with $n-1$ degrees of freedom ($t_{n-1}$): $$ \mathsf{P}\left( -t_{n-1, 1-\alpha/2} \le \frac{\overline{\mathbf{X}} - \theta}{s / \sqrt{n}} \le t_{n-1, 1-\alpha/2} \right) = 1 - \alpha $$

Solving for $\theta$: $$ -t_{n-1, 1-\alpha/2} \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \le \overline{\mathbf{X}} - \theta \le t_{n-1, 1-\alpha/2} \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} $$ $$ \overline{\mathbf{X}} - t_{n-1, 1-\alpha/2} \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \le \theta \le \overline{\mathbf{X}} + t_{n-1, 1-\alpha/2} \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} $$

Thus, the confidence interval is: $$ \mathbf{CI}_4 = \left[ \overline{\mathbf{X}} - t_{n-1, 1-\alpha/2} \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}, \, \overline{\mathbf{X}} + t_{n-1, 1-\alpha/2} \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \right] $$

theta = ID / 10.0  # theta = 1.7
alphas = [0.10, 0.05, 0.01]
ns = [10, 20, 50, 100, 300, 500, 1000]
m = 10000  # repetitions

records = []

for alpha in alphas:
    z_crit = stats.norm.ppf(1 - alpha / 2)

    for n in ns:
        # Draw exponential samples of shape (m, n)
        # scale = theta = 1/lambda
        X = np.random.exponential(scale=theta, size=(m, n))
        X_bar = X.mean(axis=1)
        s_sample = X.std(axis=1, ddof=1)

        # Quantiles
        chi2_low = stats.chi2.ppf(alpha / 2, df=2*n)
        chi2_high = stats.chi2.ppf(1 - alpha / 2, df=2*n)
        t_crit = stats.t.ppf(1 - alpha / 2, df=n-1)

        # Method 1: Chi-square exact
        ci1_low = 2 * n * X_bar / chi2_high
        ci1_high = 2 * n * X_bar / chi2_low
        cov1 = np.mean((ci1_low <= theta) & (theta <= ci1_high))
        len1 = np.mean(ci1_high - ci1_low)

        # Method 2: Normal with theta in bounds
        ci2_low = X_bar - z_crit * theta / np.sqrt(n)
        ci2_high = X_bar + z_crit * theta / np.sqrt(n)
        cov2 = np.mean((ci2_low <= theta) & (theta <= ci2_high))
        len2 = np.mean(ci2_high - ci2_low)

        # Method 3: Solved Normal inequality
        denom_low = 1 + z_crit / np.sqrt(n)
        denom_high = 1 - z_crit / np.sqrt(n)
        ci3_low = X_bar / denom_low
        ci3_high = X_bar / denom_high if denom_high > 0 else np.inf
        cov3 = np.mean((ci3_low <= theta) & (theta <= ci3_high))
        len3 = np.mean(ci3_high - ci3_low)

        # Method 4: Student t-distribution
        ci4_low = X_bar - t_crit * s_sample / np.sqrt(n)
        ci4_high = X_bar + t_crit * s_sample / np.sqrt(n)
        cov4 = np.mean((ci4_low <= theta) & (theta <= ci4_high))
        len4 = np.mean(ci4_high - ci4_low)

        records.append({
            'alpha': alpha,
            '1-alpha': 1 - alpha,
            'n': n,
            'M1 Cov': cov1, 'M1 Len': len1,
            'M2 Cov': cov2, 'M2 Len': len2,
            'M3 Cov': cov3, 'M3 Len': len3,
            'M4 Cov': cov4, 'M4 Len': len4
        })

Task (c): Effect of Sample Size $n$ and Repetitions $m$¶

  1. Effect of Sample Size $n$:

    • Empirical Coverage:
      • Method 1 ($\chi^2_{2n}$): Maintains the exact target coverage probability $1-\alpha = 0.95$ across all sample sizes (ranging from $0.9494$ at $n=10$ to $0.9517$ at $n=1000$). This confirms that $2\lambda n\overline{\mathbf{X}} \sim \chi^2_{2n}$ holds exactly for any $n$.
      • Method 2 (Normal with $\theta$): Stays very close to $0.95$ ($0.9550$ at $n=10$), but endpoints depend on the unknown $\theta$.
      • Method 3 (Solved Normal): Slightly over-covers at small $n$ ($0.9550$ at $n=10$) due to asymmetric expansion of the upper bound, rapidly converging to $0.9514$ as $n \to 1000$.
      • Method 4 (Student $t$): Significantly undercovers for small sample sizes ($0.9000$ at $n=10$ and $0.9187$ at $n=20$). This happens because Student's $t$-distribution assumes underlying normality, whereas $\mathscr{E}(\lambda)$ is heavily right-skewed. As $n$ grows ($n \ge 500$), the Central Limit Theorem takes effect and coverage reaches $0.9514$.
    • Interval Length:
      • For all 4 methods, the average interval length shrinks at the rate of $\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{n})$.
      • At $n=10$, Method 3 yields the widest interval ($3.3948$), while Method 4 yields the narrowest ($2.2342$) at the cost of failing the coverage target.
      • At $n=1000$, all four methods converge to virtually identical interval lengths ($\approx 0.211$).

  2. Effect of Repetitions $m$:

    • $m$ is the number of simulation runs.
    • It does not alter the underlying theoretical coverage or expected interval length.
    • Higher values of $m$ (e.g., $m=10\,000$) reduce estimation noise, yielding smooth curves and high precision with a standard error of $\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{m}} \approx 0.22\%$.

Task (d): Comparison of all 4 Methods¶

Method Pros Cons Best Use Case
1. Exact $\chi^2_{2n}$ • Exact coverage ($0.95$) for any $n$.
• Fully accounts for exponential skewness.		 • Specific only to the exponential distribution. Best overall when data is known to be exponential.
2. Normal w/ $\theta$ • Simple theoretical structure. • Impractical (depends on unknown parameter $\theta$). Theoretical reference only.
3. Solved Normal • Parameter-free bounds.
• Asymmetric bounds naturally accommodate right skewness.		 • Relies on asymptotic normality of $\overline{\mathbf{X}}$. Best practical alternative when exact distribution is unknown.
4. Student $t$ • Universal (model-free) method. • Undercovers for small $n$ on skewed distributions ($90.0\%$ at $n=10$). Recommended only for large samples ($n \ge 100$).

Conclusion¶

  1. Exactness of Method 1: Empirical coverage matches $1-\alpha = 0.95$ across all $n$, proving the exactness of the $\chi^2_{2n}$ distribution for $2\lambda n\overline{\mathbf{X}}$.
  2. Inadequacy of Student's $t$ for small skewed samples: Method 4 severely underperforms at small $n$ ($90.0\%$ coverage for $n=10$), demonstrating that $t$-intervals should not be used for small, non-normal samples.
  3. Utility of Method 3: Solving the normal inequality yields a robust practical confidence interval that preserves target coverage even for small $n$.
  4. Asymptotic Convergence: For $n \ge 300$, all practical methods (Methods 1, 3, and 4) become practically indistinguishable in both coverage ($\approx 95\%$) and interval length ($\approx 0.211$ at $n=1000$).

Problem 2¶

Repeat parts (2)–(4) of Problem 1 (with the corresponding amendments) for a Poisson distribution $\mathscr{P}(\theta)$.

The Task stays the same: derive the intervals, check that they contain the parameter $\theta$ with the prescribed probability, find their lengths, compare the methods and write your conclusions.

Solution¶

(a) Derivation of Confidence Intervals¶

For a Poisson random variable $X_i \sim \mathcal{P}(\theta)$, the expected value and variance are:

$$ E(X_i) = \theta, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_i) = \theta. $$

The sample mean is defined as:

$$ \overline{X} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i. $$

By the linearity of expectation:

$$ E(\overline{X}) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}E(X_i) = \frac{n\theta}{n} = \theta. $$

Since the observations are independent:

$$ \operatorname{Var}(\overline{X}) = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Var}(X_i) = \frac{n\theta}{n^2} = \frac{\theta}{n}. $$

Therefore,

$$ \boxed{ E(\overline{X}) = \theta, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\overline{X}) = \frac{\theta}{n} } $$

By the Central Limit Theorem, for sufficiently large $n$, the sample mean is approximately normally distributed:

$$ \overline{X} \approx \mathcal{N}\left(\theta, \frac{\theta}{n}\right). $$

We standardize the sample mean:

$$ Z_n = \frac{\overline{X} - E(\overline{X})} {\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\overline{X})}}. $$

Since $E(\overline{X}) = \theta$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\overline{X}) = \theta/n$, we obtain:

$$ Z_n = \frac{\overline{X} - \theta}{\sqrt{\theta/n}} = \frac{\sqrt{n}(\overline{X} - \theta)}{\sqrt{\theta}}. $$

Therefore,

$$ \boxed{ Z_n = \frac{\sqrt{n}(\overline{X} - \theta)}{\sqrt{\theta}} \approx \mathcal{N}(0,1) } $$

Let $1-\alpha$ be the desired confidence level and define

$$ \beta = 1 - \frac{\alpha}{2}. $$

Let $z_\beta$ denote the $\beta$-quantile of the standard normal distribution.

By the symmetry of the standard normal distribution,

$$ P(-z_\beta \le Z \le z_\beta) = \Phi(z_\beta) - \Phi(-z_\beta), $$

where $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ and $\Phi$ is its cumulative distribution function.

Since

$$ \Phi(z_\beta) = \beta $$

and

$$ \Phi(-z_\beta) = 1-\beta, $$

we obtain

$$ P(-z_\beta \le Z \le z_\beta) = \beta - (1-\beta) = 2\beta-1 = 1-\alpha. $$

Using the normal approximation for $Z_n$,

$$ \boxed{ P(-z_\beta \le Z_n \le z_\beta) \approx 1-\alpha. } $$

Substituting the expression for $Z_n$, we obtain:

$$ P\left( -z_\beta \le \frac{\sqrt{n}(\overline{X}-\theta)}{\sqrt{\theta}} \le z_\beta \right) \approx 1-\alpha. $$

Since $\theta>0$ and $n>0$, multiplying the inequality by $\sqrt{\theta/n}$ gives:

$$ P\left( -z_\beta\sqrt{\frac{\theta}{n}} \le \overline{X}-\theta \le z_\beta\sqrt{\frac{\theta}{n}} \right) \approx 1-\alpha. $$

Rearranging the inequality:

$$ P\left( \overline{X}-z_\beta\sqrt{\frac{\theta}{n}} \le \theta \le \overline{X}+z_\beta\sqrt{\frac{\theta}{n}} \right) \approx 1-\alpha. $$

Therefore, the interval obtained using the normal approximation is:

$$ \boxed{ I_2 = \left[ \overline{X}-z_\beta\sqrt{\frac{\theta}{n}}, \quad \overline{X}+z_\beta\sqrt{\frac{\theta}{n}} \right] } $$

The length of the interval is the difference between its upper and lower bounds:

$$ \ell_2 = \left(\overline{X}+z_\beta\sqrt{\frac{\theta}{n}}\right) - \left(\overline{X}-z_\beta\sqrt{\frac{\theta}{n}}\right). $$

Simplifying, we obtain:

$$ \boxed{ \ell_2 = 2z_\beta\sqrt{\frac{\theta}{n}} } $$

Method 3. Solving the Confidence Inequality¶

The interval obtained in Method 2 depends on the unknown parameter $\theta$.

To eliminate this dependence, we solve the inequality

$$ |\overline{X}-\theta| \le z_\beta\sqrt{\frac{\theta}{n}} $$

for $\theta$.

Since both sides are non-negative, we can square the inequality:

$$ (\overline{X}-\theta)^2 \le \frac{z_\beta^2\theta}{n}. $$

Expanding the square:

$$ \overline{X}^2-2\overline{X}\theta+\theta^2 \le \frac{z_\beta^2\theta}{n}. $$

Rearranging the terms:

$$ \boxed{ \theta^2- \left(2\overline{X}+\frac{z_\beta^2}{n}\right)\theta +\overline{X}^2 \le 0 } $$ To solve the quadratic inequality, we calculate the discriminant:

$$ D = \left(2\overline{X}+\frac{z_\beta^2}{n}\right)^2 -4\overline{X}^2 = \frac{4\overline{X}z_\beta^2}{n} +\frac{z_\beta^4}{n^2}. $$

The roots of the corresponding quadratic equation are:

$$ \theta_{1,2} = \frac{ 2\overline{X}+\frac{z_\beta^2}{n} \pm\sqrt{D} }{2}. $$

Simplifying, we obtain:

$$ \theta_{1,2} = \overline{X}+\frac{z_\beta^2}{2n} \pm z_\beta \sqrt{ \frac{\overline{X}}{n} +\frac{z_\beta^2}{4n^2} }. $$

Since the coefficient of $\theta^2$ is positive, the quadratic inequality holds between the two roots.

Therefore, the confidence interval is:

$$ \boxed{ I_3= \left[ \overline{X}+\frac{z_\beta^2}{2n} -z_\beta\sqrt{\frac{\overline{X}}{n}+\frac{z_\beta^2}{4n^2}}, \quad \overline{X}+\frac{z_\beta^2}{2n} +z_\beta\sqrt{\frac{\overline{X}}{n}+\frac{z_\beta^2}{4n^2}} \right] } $$

Unlike Method 2, this interval does not depend on the unknown parameter $\theta$ and can be calculated directly from the sample.

$$ \begin{aligned} \ell_3 ={}& \left( \overline{X}+\frac{z_\beta^2}{2n} +z_\beta\sqrt{\frac{\overline{X}}{n}+\frac{z_\beta^2}{4n^2}} \right)\\ &- \left( \overline{X}+\frac{z_\beta^2}{2n} -z_\beta\sqrt{\frac{\overline{X}}{n}+\frac{z_\beta^2}{4n^2}} \right) \end{aligned} $$

Simplifying, we obtain:

$$ \boxed{ \ell_3= 2z_\beta\sqrt{\frac{\overline{X}}{n}+\frac{z_\beta^2}{4n^2}} } $$

Method 4. Student's t-distribution¶

In Method 2, the standard deviation $\sqrt{\theta}$ is unknown. We estimate it using the sample observations.

Define the sum of squared deviations:

$$ S_{XX}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i-\overline{X})^2. $$

The sample variance is:

$$ \widehat{\sigma}^2=\frac{S_{XX}}{n-1}. $$

We define the studentized statistic:

$$ T= \frac{\sqrt{n}(\overline{X}-\theta)} {\sqrt{S_{XX}/(n-1)}}. $$

Simplifying:

$$ \boxed{ T=\sqrt{n(n-1)} \frac{\overline{X}-\theta}{\sqrt{S_{XX}}} } $$

For normally distributed observations, this statistic has Student's t-distribution with $n-1$ degrees of freedom.

Since our observations follow a Poisson distribution, we use the approximation:

$$ T\approx T_{n-1}. $$

Let $t_{\beta,n-1}$ denote the $\beta$-quantile of Student's t-distribution with $n-1$ degrees of freedom.

Using the symmetry of this distribution:

$$ P(-t_{\beta,n-1}\le T\le t_{\beta,n-1}) \approx 1-\alpha. $$

Substituting the expression for $T$:

$$ P\left( -t_{\beta,n-1} \le \sqrt{n(n-1)} \frac{\overline{X}-\theta}{\sqrt{S_{XX}}} \le t_{\beta,n-1} \right) \approx 1-\alpha. $$

For $S_{XX}>0$, multiplying the inequality by $\sqrt{S_{XX}/(n(n-1))}$ gives:

$$ P\left( -t_{\beta,n-1}\sqrt{\frac{S_{XX}}{n(n-1)}} \le \overline{X}-\theta \le t_{\beta,n-1}\sqrt{\frac{S_{XX}}{n(n-1)}} \right) \approx 1-\alpha. $$

Rearranging the inequality:

$$ P\left( \overline{X}-t_{\beta,n-1}\sqrt{\frac{S_{XX}}{n(n-1)}} \le \theta \le \overline{X}+t_{\beta,n-1}\sqrt{\frac{S_{XX}}{n(n-1)}} \right) \approx 1-\alpha. $$

Therefore, the confidence interval is:

$$ \boxed{ I_4= \left[ \overline{X}-t_{\beta,n-1}\sqrt{\frac{S_{XX}}{n(n-1)}}, \quad \overline{X}+t_{\beta,n-1}\sqrt{\frac{S_{XX}}{n(n-1)}} \right] } $$

The length of the interval is:

$$ \ell_4= \left( \overline{X}+t_{\beta,n-1}\sqrt{\frac{S_{XX}}{n(n-1)}} \right) - \left( \overline{X}-t_{\beta,n-1}\sqrt{\frac{S_{XX}}{n(n-1)}} \right). $$

Simplifying:

$$ \boxed{ \ell_4= 2t_{\beta,n-1}\sqrt{\frac{S_{XX}}{n(n-1)}} } $$

The interval can be calculated from the sample without knowing the true parameter $\theta$.

def simulate_intervals(n, m, alpha):
    samples = np.random.poisson(theta, size=(m, n))
    means = np.mean(samples, axis=1)

    beta = 1 - alpha / 2
    z_beta = stats.norm.ppf(beta)
    t_beta = stats.t.ppf(beta, df=n - 1)

    # Method 2
    margin_2 = z_beta * np.sqrt(theta / n)

    lower_2 = means - margin_2
    upper_2 = means + margin_2

    # Method 3
    center_3 = means + z_beta**2 / (2 * n)
    margin_3 = z_beta * np.sqrt(
        means / n + z_beta**2 / (4 * n**2)
    )

    lower_3 = center_3 - margin_3
    upper_3 = center_3 + margin_3

    # Method 4
    S_XX = np.sum((samples - means[:, None])**2, axis=1)

    margin_4 = t_beta * np.sqrt(S_XX / (n * (n - 1)))

    lower_4 = means - margin_4
    upper_4 = means + margin_4

    results = {}

    for method, lower, upper in [
        (2, lower_2, upper_2),
        (3, lower_3, upper_3),
        (4, lower_4, upper_4)
    ]:
        coverage = np.mean((lower <= theta) & (theta <= upper))
        average_length = np.mean(upper - lower)

        results[method] = (coverage, average_length)

    return results

(c) Effect of $n$ and $m$¶

Sample size $n$. As $n$ grows, the intervals become shorter: their length is proportional to $1/\sqrt{n}$ (e.g. $\ell_2 = 2z_\beta\sqrt{\theta/n}$). The actual confidence gets closer to $1-\alpha$, because the normal approximation of $\overline{X}$ (CLT) becomes more accurate for larger $n$.

Number of repetitions $m$. The value of $m$ does not change the intervals themselves, it only affects how accurately we estimate the actual confidence. For small $m$ (e.g. $m=100$) the estimated coverage fluctuates noticeably around $1-\alpha$, while for large $m$ ($m=5000$) it is stable and close to the true coverage probability.

(d) Comparison of methods¶

  • Method 2 gives the shortest interval, but it uses the unknown parameter $\theta$, so it cannot be used in practice.
  • Method 3 has exactly the same coverage as Method 2. This is because $\theta \in I_3$ is the same event as $|\overline{X}-\theta| \le z_\beta\sqrt{\theta/n}$, which is just solved for $\theta$. Unlike Method 2, it does not depend on $\theta$, and its length is only slightly larger. This makes it the best method for the Poisson distribution.
  • Method 4 is universal, because it does not use the fact that $\operatorname{Var}(X_i)=\theta$. However, for small $n$ its intervals are slightly longer and its coverage is slightly below $1-\alpha$, since the $t$-approximation is not exact for Poisson data.

For large $n$ all three methods give practically the same results.

Conclusion¶

We derived three confidence intervals for the parameter $\theta$ of the Poisson distribution $\mathcal{P}(\theta)$ and checked them by simulation with $\theta = 1.7$.

For all three methods the actual confidence is close to the nominal level $1-\alpha$ for every $\alpha \in \{0.1, 0.05, 0.01\}$. As the sample size $n$ grows, the intervals become shorter (their length decreases as $1/\sqrt{n}$) and the actual confidence gets closer to $1-\alpha$. The number of repetitions $m$ does not affect the intervals, it only determines how accurately we estimate the actual confidence: the larger $m$, the more stable the result.

Method 2 cannot be used in practice because it depends on the unknown $\theta$. Method 3 has exactly the same coverage as Method 2, but does not require $\theta$, and its length is only slightly larger, so it is the best method for the Poisson distribution. Method 4 is more universal, but for small $n$ its coverage is slightly below $1-\alpha$ and its intervals are slightly longer. For large $n$ all methods give practically the same results.

(d) In (b), one sample gives very different values, because one sample is random. So we need the averages from (c) to see the bias.

For small $n$ (e.g. $n=10$), $\sigma^2_n$ is clearly too small on average (bias $\approx -0.4$). The bias of $\sigma^2_{n-1}$ is close to $0$ for every $n$. When $n$ grows, the bias of $\sigma^2_n$ goes to $0$. At $n = 1000$ the two estimators are almost the same.

(e) Use $\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i - \overline{X})^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i^2 - n\overline{X}^2$.

We know that $E(X_i^2) = \sigma^2 + \mu^2$ and $E(\overline{X}^2) = \mathrm{Var}(\overline{X}) + \mu^2 = \dfrac{\sigma^2}{n} + \mu^2$. So

$$E\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i - \overline{X})^2\Big] = n(\sigma^2 + \mu^2) - n\Big(\frac{\sigma^2}{n} + \mu^2\Big) = (n-1)\sigma^2.$$

Therefore:

$$E(\sigma^2_n) = \frac{(n-1)\sigma^2}{n} = \sigma^2 - \frac{\sigma^2}{n}, \qquad E(\sigma^2_{n-1}) = \frac{(n-1)\sigma^2}{n-1} = \sigma^2.$$

(f)

$$Bias(\sigma^2_n) = -\frac{\sigma^2}{n} \neq 0, \qquad Bias(\sigma^2_{n-1}) = 0.$$

So $\sigma^2_{n-1}$ is unbiased and $\sigma^2_n$ is biased (it underestimates). The bias of $\sigma^2_n$ goes to $0$ as $n \to \infty$, so it is only asymptotically unbiased.

Why: $\overline{X}$ is computed from the same data, so the points are always a bit closer to $\overline{X}$ than to $\mu$. We lose one degree of freedom, so we divide by $n-1$.

Conclusion¶

(g) The simulation agrees with the theory:

  • $\sigma^2_{n-1}$ is unbiased: its average is $\approx \sigma^2 = 4$ for every $n$.
  • $\sigma^2_n$ underestimates $\sigma^2$ by about $\sigma^2/n$ (e.g. $-0.4$ for $n=10$).
  • For large $n$ the difference is very small, so both estimators work. For small $n$ we should use $\sigma^2_{n-1}$.
